*How the attention economy is reshaping institutional trust and creating an emerging information security risk.*

A criminal trial takes place within the walls of a courtroom. Today, another proceeding takes place simultaneously on millions of connected devices. It has its own investigators, experts, financial incentives and, frequently, its own verdict.

This parallel court operates without the formal rules of evidence and procedural safeguards that govern a criminal trial. Its influence can extend beyond the digital environment in which it exists.

Two recent Massachusetts criminal cases offer an opportunity to examine what happens when the American judicial system intersects with the modern information economy, and why the consequences should concern policymakers, judicial officials, technology companies and the national security community.

## A tale of two trials

The prosecution of Karen Read in connection with the death of Boston police officer John O'Keefe developed into a national media event. Independent publishers, social media personalities and members of the public scrutinized evidence, challenged investigative conclusions and organized around competing explanations.

There were legitimate grounds for scrutiny. In March 2025, the lead investigator, Massachusetts State Police trooper Michael Proctor, was [fired following disciplinary proceedings](https://apnews.com/article/dd539babdc2f5509a81be9dbed0967ad). On June 18, 2025, Read was [acquitted of murder and manslaughter and convicted of operating under the influence](https://apnews.com/article/e636f03f40f3c03f24338d5ba7d40bd1).

The case illustrates the importance of public oversight and the risks that accompany a large-scale participatory investigation. A blogger who advocated for Read also faced witness intimidation charges, as reported by the Associated Press. Charges are allegations, not findings of guilt. The investigator's misconduct and Read's acquittal do not establish that a broader conspiracy occurred.

A year later, the trial of Lindsay Clancy attracted another online following. The central legal question was whether she was criminally responsible for the deaths of her children. On September 4, 2026, the court [declared a mistrial after jurors could not reach a unanimous verdict](https://www.wgbh.org/news/local/2026-09-04/mistrial-formally-declared-in-lindsay-clancy-trial-after-last-minute-appeal-is-denied).

Despite significant differences between the cases, familiar patterns emerged. Online communities examined testimony, developed alternative explanations and circulated unsubstantiated allegations. In [reporting on the Clancy case](https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/17/lindsay-clancy-trial-conspiracy-theories-postpartum-psychosis), Boston University media science researcher Katy Coduto described the phenomenon as forensic fandom.

The distinction between watching a criminal proceeding and participating in one is becoming increasingly blurred. Whether the same audiences or influential accounts migrated between these cases is a question for empirical research, not a finding established by these examples.

## The economics of the parallel court

Understanding this phenomenon requires examining the environment in which it operates.

Social media platforms compete for human attention. Content creators compete for distribution within those platforms. Audiences provide engagement signals that can influence which material receives additional exposure.

These incentives are not necessarily aligned with the evidentiary standards of a criminal investigation.

A measured explanation of why available evidence remains inconclusive may compete poorly for attention against a confident assertion that investigators are concealing something. A correction issued days later may never reach the people who encountered the original allegation.

A [2021 study in Science Advances](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abe5641) examined 12.7 million tweets and conducted two behavioral experiments. The researchers found that social rewards for expressing moral outrage were associated with subsequent expressions of outrage, with experimental evidence supporting the reinforcement mechanism.

The implications extend beyond any individual content creator. The information environment itself can influence the behavior of its participants.

A community initially organized around a legitimate concern can consequently develop narratives that are difficult to reconcile with the available evidence.

The concern is not that citizens are examining criminal cases. Public scrutiny is essential to institutional accountability. The concern is that the systems distributing information can reward its emotional impact independently of its reliability.

## An emerging information attack surface

In cybersecurity, an attack surface describes the points through which a system might be compromised.

We should consider institutional trust in similar terms. The American judicial system depends on witnesses being willing to testify, jurors being able to deliberate independently and public officials being held accountable when they fail to meet their responsibilities.

Each of these dependencies represents a potential vulnerability.

Existing controversies can create opportunities for deceptive influence. [Research by Kate Starbird, Ahmer Arif and Tom Wilson](https://doi.org/10.1145/3359229) examines how strategic information operations interact with participatory online communities. It provides a framework for understanding how organized and organic activity may coexist.

This is not evidence that a foreign government interfered in the Read or Clancy cases. No such attribution has been established in this preliminary review. Nor does the similarity of online narratives establish that their authors coordinated.

It is a reason to examine the resilience of an information environment in which localized controversies can acquire national significance.

## When institutional distrust is deserved

There is a danger in framing this problem exclusively as one of disinformation or foreign interference.

Institutions sometimes fail. Investigators engage in misconduct. Prosecutors make mistakes. Judicial systems can produce unjust outcomes.

Independent journalism and public scrutiny have historically helped expose these failures.

Efforts to preserve institutional trust that suppress legitimate criticism would undermine the very institutions they intend to protect.

We should also avoid assuming that confidence in courts is collapsing nationwide. In the [National Center for State Courts' 2025 public opinion survey](https://www.ncsc.org/resources-courts/state-state-courts-2025-public-opinion-poll-findings), 62 percent of respondents expressed some or a great deal of trust in their state court system. A national survey cannot establish how particular cases or online communities affect local trust.

The objective should not be to manufacture confidence in the judicial system. It should be to establish conditions under which citizens can reasonably have confidence in it.

Transparency, procedural fairness and accountability are therefore integral to information resilience.

## Protecting the judicial process

For the judiciary, the challenge includes managing exposure to information outside the courtroom.

In 2020, the [federal judiciary issued updated model jury instructions](https://www.uscourts.gov/data-news/judiciary-news/2020/10/01/new-jury-instructions-strengthen-social-media-cautions) addressing social media. Modern recommendation systems complicate the problem: a juror may encounter information about a proceeding without actively searching for it.

Courts should develop practical guidance for these circumstances, including procedures through which jurors can confidentially report inadvertent exposure. Witnesses and jurors should also have accessible mechanisms for reporting credible threats and harassment.

Court administrators could reduce information gaps by making lawfully public records, rulings and explanations of procedural developments more accessible. Such resources would not replace independent journalism but would provide journalists and citizens with a common evidentiary reference. Privacy, sealed records and fair-trial safeguards must remain intact.

For technology companies, the challenge is distinguishing legitimate public participation from deceptive manipulation.

Credible threats, impersonation and evidence of coordinated inauthentic activity warrant investigation. Criticism of government institutions, advocacy for defendants and disagreement with judicial decisions should not independently trigger intervention. Platforms should provide clear enforcement standards, meaningful appeals and transparency about their decisions.

Policymakers can contribute by supporting independent research into the distribution and societal consequences of online information. Researchers need access to appropriate platform data, subject to safeguards for privacy, free expression and due process. The European Union's [Digital Services Act research-access framework](https://algorithmic-transparency.ec.europa.eu/news/faqs-dsa-data-access-researchers-2025-07-03_en) offers one precedent for studying systemic platform risks, not a ready-made prescription for American law.

These measures should be evaluated against their actual effects on public understanding, participant safety and procedural fairness, rather than on the amount of criticism they suppress.

## From hypothesis to evidence

HybridSec proposes a comparative study of public information ecosystems surrounding high-profile criminal proceedings. Its initial questions are whether public creator networks recur across cases, whether claims persist after corrections, and whether observable information activity corresponds with independently documented institutional harms.

A credible study must include comparison cases, distinguish news-driven surges from anomalous activity and account for preexisting distrust. It must not infer coordination from shared hashtags, overlapping audiences or synchronized responses to court events alone. Trust should be measured directly through appropriate surveys or other validated methods, rather than inferred from post volume or sentiment.

The research would focus on public content using authorized access methods, avoid identifying private jurors or witnesses, and distinguish organic advocacy from evidence of deception. Platform-level reach and recommendation data, where available, would be needed to test amplification hypotheses.

No original account-level dataset or causal estimate of trust erosion is presented here. The two cases motivate the investigation; they do not establish a coordinated campaign.

## Trust as a component of national resilience

The parallel court is a manifestation of a broader transformation in how societies acquire information, evaluate evidence and assign legitimacy to institutions.

The same underlying mechanisms may affect public health, emergency response and confidence in other essential government services.

The United States cannot eliminate disagreement, nor should it attempt to. The ability to examine and correct institutional failures is itself a source of democratic resilience.

The challenge is ensuring that the information systems through which those failures are examined do not become mechanisms for intimidating participants, misleading the public or interfering with the processes intended to deliver justice.

The American judicial system has developed safeguards against improper influence inside the courtroom. We must now understand how to preserve those safeguards in an information environment that extends far beyond its walls.
